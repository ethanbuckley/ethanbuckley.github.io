Research

Two studies of language development

Both studies are Bayesian; each has a plain-language explainer and a more technical write-up.

Study 01 · Vocabulary growth

How children with Down syndrome build vocabulary

A pooled Bayesian study of the words children understand, say and sign. It draws on parent-report checklists from fourteen datasets in six countries, put onto a common scale.

Production ratio from a VG14 test-tier fit of 4 September 2026  ·  14 datasets  ·  1,424 assessments  ·  reported to 6 years  ·  comparisons with typically-developing children from the July 2026 fits  ·  Preliminary findings

Understanding runs years ahead of speaking

The production ratio is the share of understood words a child also says. It climbs steeply with age, reaching about 44% by age four and 64% by six, the oldest age the model reports.

Spoken vocabulary lags furthest

By around age two, most children with Down syndrome say fewer words than the lowest-scoring tenth of typically-developing children. Understanding is delayed too, but by less.

The gap is wider than in typical development, then narrows

At the same level of comprehension, children with Down syndrome say a smaller share of the words they know. The difference peaks at around 13 percentage points at 50–150 understood words, then fades as vocabulary grows.

Signing offers a modest early bridge

Counting signed words credits children with expressive vocabulary they already have, and narrows the early gap somewhat. The narrowing is concentrated around ages two to three; the spoken-word gap stays large.

My contribution

I built the signing and within-child longitudinal models, the pipeline that harmonises the studies in DuckDB, and the descriptive and comprehension–production-gap reports. In September 2026 I wrote the report’s data chapter: what each of the fourteen sources collected, and how counts from checklists of different lengths are scored against the 810-word inventory. The pool has grown since these figures were fitted, to 777 children after the data checks.

Study 02 · Reading & language

What a reading-and-language intervention improved

A re-analysis of the RLI trial (Burgoyne et al., 2012). 54 children with Down syndrome were randomly assigned to daily one-to-one reading-and-language teaching, or to a waiting list. The analysis is built around a causal diagram, which keeps the effect of the teaching separate from patterns that just track a child’s general ability.

RLI randomised trial  ·  54 children  ·  4 assessment waves  ·  only the randomised effects are causal  ·  Preliminary findings

The teaching credibly improved decoding

Letter-sound knowledge, blending and word reading all rose. The specific words children were taught to say also improved.

Broad vocabulary and grammar did not move

Gains faded with distance from what was taught: skills close to the instruction improved, distant ones didn’t. That matches the original trial’s own conclusion.

Everything beyond the randomised contrast is association

Where a child starts predicts a lot about where they end up. But which children progress fastest stays confounded with general ability, so it’s reported as a pattern, never a cause.

My contribution

My part was the intention-to-treat, difference-in-differences, mediation and mechanism models inside the study’s causal DAG, plus the gradient-boosting layer that ranks candidate predictors and the results reporting. Between June and August 2026 that came to 124 merged pull requests.

How to read these results

Both studies are exploratory and the findings are preliminary; the estimates will sharpen as more data arrive. Every quantity is a posterior distribution, so it’s a credible range, not a point. And apart from the randomised teaching effects in the RLI trial, these are associations, not proven causes.

Tooling for teaching materials

The production system behind DSE’s teaching materials: images, spoken audio and short video clips, and the route from a generated file to a catalogued asset. It started as an evaluation harness, because the first job was deciding which model to trust.

Down Syndrome Education International · production system

Teaching-image pipeline

A pipeline producing photographic teaching images for an early-vocabulary programme: single words, phrases, sentences and a set of named characters who recur across the set. Children with Down syndrome commonly have reduced contrast sensitivity, so the specification picks each background against its subject: the object keeps its true colour and the background moves, which is what stops a white bath dissolving into the backdrop.

A wrong image gets a glossary fix; a wrong class of images gets a master-prompt rule that also governs the words not yet generated. Batch inference halved the unit cost, and reruns are idempotent by request hash.

First production set delivered July 2026: 7,351 images · review so far is by eye, not by an automated check: no claim is made about image quality

Down Syndrome Education International · production system

Spoken-word audio pipeline

A pipeline that reads the curriculum aloud: 3,407 words, phrases and sentences, each recorded by a male and a female voice in four English accents. The voices were chosen in a blind A/B listening arena over four commercial text-to-speech providers, ranked by Bradley–Terry with bootstrap confidence intervals, then brought to one measured speaking pace by calibration, because the same speed setting gives different voices different paces.

The scorer reports 90% intervals and flags which voices are statistically indistinguishable from the leader. I found and fixed one scoring bug: tie-credit had been inventing leaders out of dead heats.

Eight accents screened to sixteen voices; four accents shipped, four parked · many short-phrase comparisons tie, so the middle of the ranking stays unresolved

Down Syndrome Education International · production system

Verb video clips

Short clips that teach verbs: a toddler brushing, eating, sleeping. Through the video model’s API the same prompt failed on every tier, with two toothbrushes, broken physics and foam everywhere; in the model’s own web tool it worked. So clips are made there by hand, and the pipeline does the part a hand cannot: it holds one source of truth for every prompt, renders the paste-sheet from it, and refuses to file a clip whose brief has since changed.

That rule came from a real drift. Four of the eight validated prompts no longer matched the wording that had been approved, because the sheet a person pastes from was a hand-written copy. Now it is generated, and a test fails when it is stale.

Prompts drafted for about sixty verbs; eight validated · provenance records the tool used, not a guessed model tier

Down Syndrome Education International · production system

From clips on a disk to catalogued assets

The route from a rendered clip to a draft asset in the charity’s catalogue: audit by measurement and by ear, repair, upload, reconcile. A defect found by listening is fixed by promoting a repaired take, never by regenerating: without the promotion stamp, the next render puts the defect back over the fix and reports success.

Upload consumes an approved inventory, never a directory scan, and every row carries its content hash, which is also the key the reconciler joins on. Four accents of curriculum audio are in the catalogue as drafts. Publishing them is a separate human decision.

23,846 draft assets reconciled clean · stalls traced to hung TCP connects, so calls time out at 20 to 30 seconds · an expired session now stops the run instead of being retried for seven hours

  • SHA-256
  • reconciliation
  • timeouts and retries

Independent project

S&P 500 stock screener

A two-stage screener for the S&P 500. An XGBoost classifier, trained on ~1.3 million daily observations, ranks long and short candidates, and FinBERT reads the news sentiment on the top picks. Labels use the triple-barrier method to avoid look-ahead. In walk-forward testing from 2020 to 2026 the top-15 picks were right about 48% of the time, against a 29% base rate. A Streamlit dashboard runs it live, with a Monte-Carlo VaR/CVaR risk simulator.

Expanding-window validation · limitations reported: survivorship bias, no transaction costs

  • FinBERT

Approach

How I work

I fit models with MCMC and report full posteriors. I use machine learning to find structure in larger datasets, and I keep the work in tested, version-controlled code so results reproduce.

Bayesian hierarchical modelling: multilevel models with interpretable priors; study- and child-level random effects. Gaussian processes: scalable Hilbert-space (HSGP) smooths for age trajectories. PyMC & NUTS sampling: full-posterior MCMC via nutpie, gated on R̂, effective sample size and divergences. Model checking & comparison: prior and posterior predictive checks; PSIS-LOO and leave-one-study-out cross-validation.

Causal inference & DAGs: randomised effects identified by design; confounded paths reported as association. Gradient boosting & NLP: XGBoost and LightGBM with grouped cross-validation and SHAP; FinBERT for financial-news sentiment. Generative-model evaluation: blind A/B comparison with Bradley–Terry ranking and bootstrap intervals; prompts written as a specification.

Scientific computing & simulation: vectorised simulations, like a spatial epidemic cellular automaton, validated against their analytical limits; ~40× speedups. Data engineering: eleven studies harmonised in a DuckDB view, with validation and ceiling guards. Reproducible pipelines: raw data → posteriors → figures → report, reproducible at three sampling tiers and tested in CI.

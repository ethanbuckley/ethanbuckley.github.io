My stock-ranking model did no better than sorting by volatility.

For two months my S&P 500 screener looked like it worked. A one-line baseline showed that almost everything it had learned was how volatile each stock was.

What I built

The screener ranks S&P 500 stocks each day. An XGBoost classifier scores every stock, and FinBERT, a language model trained on financial text, scores news sentiment for the shortlist. A Streamlit dashboard shows the latest picks.

The classifier learns from a label built with the triple-barrier method. For each stock and day, I look five trading days ahead. If the price rises 4% before it falls 4%, the label is 1. If it falls 4% first, or touches neither barrier, the label is 0.

I tested it walk-forward: train on the past, predict the next year, then move the window on. The test covers 1,659 trading days from January 2020 to September 2026.

The result that looked good

On an average day, 28.8% of stocks hit the upper barrier first. Of the model’s top 15 picks, 46.0% did. The picks beat the base rate on 77% of test days. In July the project’s README said the ranking added value in every regime tested.

The baseline

In September I reviewed the project with an AI coding assistant. One of the checks we added was the simplest possible competitor: rank each day’s stocks by a single number and use no model at all.

The number was the ATR ratio. ATR, average true range, is the average size of a stock’s daily price range, including overnight gaps, over the last 14 days. Dividing by the closing price makes it comparable across stocks. It is a plain measure of how much a stock moves.

Ranking by the ATR ratio alone scored 46.1%. The model scored 46.0%. Across the whole ranking the two were also level. The classifier’s ranking performed no better than a simple volatility measure. The walk-forward test covers the classifier only, so it says nothing either way about the FinBERT news stage, which I have not validated.

Why volatility wins under this label

The label rewards volatility by construction. A volatile stock is likely to hit one of the two barriers within five days. A quiet stock often hits neither, and those windows are labelled 0. So the stocks most likely to be labelled 1 are the ones that move most. The model learned exactly what the label asked for.

Did it beat the index?

No. The backtest buys each day’s top 15 at the close, holds them for five trading days whatever happens, and charges 10 basis points (0.1%) each way.

The picks made money, 11.7% a year after costs, but less than simply holding the index, and with almost twice its volatility. The hit rate counts how often a stock rose 4% before it fell 4%. It says nothing about how far the picks moved over the five days they were actually held, and volatile stocks move a long way in both directions.

One more caveat. The stock universe is rebuilt from the index’s change history, but price data survives for only 124 of the 242 stocks removed since the start. The firms that dropped out, often after falling, are partly missing, so all of these numbers are somewhat flattering.

What it changed

  • I run the dumbest baseline first. Before training anything, I now rank by the one or two obvious variables and record the score. A model has to beat that, not the base rate.
  • I read the label before the model. The question is what the cheapest way to score well on this label is. Here it was “pick volatile stocks”, and a powerful model will always find the cheapest way.
  • I report the negative result. The project README and this site now lead with the finding, not with the 46%.

If I return to the screener, the first change is the label. I would test volatility-scaled barriers to reduce the label’s preference for volatile stocks, then run the ATR baseline again before trusting any improvement.
